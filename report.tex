\begin{abstract}
We compare ways of reducing transformer depth on the same target blocks of
the same models: \emph{deleting} them, \emph{replacing} them with a fitted
rank-32 ternary low-rank bridge, \emph{deleting then repairing} with pruned
distillation, and \emph{fine-tuning with LayerDrop}. Every configuration is
repeated over multiple seeds and reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation;
the fitting procedure is bit-level deterministic, verified rather than
assumed; and compression is measured in parameters, fp16 bytes and wall-clock
latency rather than asserted.

Two results survive scrutiny. First, the best method is
\emph{model-dependent}: BI-targeted removal plus distillation wins on GPT-2
($41.68 \pm 0.37$ against a $38.25$ baseline), while LayerDrop fine-tuning
wins on SmolLM-135M and is the only configuration in the entire study that
lands \emph{below} its own unpruned baseline ($15.54 \pm 0.01$ against
$16.36$). Second, a negative result: a fitted low-rank bridge loses to plain
deletion while achieving \emph{identical} parameter counts, so it deletes the
same weights and then adds a perturbation on top. A training-free SVD
diagnostic explains why, and shows that block influence and residual
low-rankness are different properties, and that improving the bridge's local
layer-wise fit makes end-task perplexity \emph{worse}.

All numbers are produced by a single self-auditing notebook
(\texttt{assignment2\_tpu.ipynb}) which refuses to certify its own output
unless thirteen structural invariants hold.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{Baselines and related work}
We position against the work that is runnable on our compute budget.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{ShortGPT} (Men et al., 2024) introduces Block Influence, the
exact metric we use for target selection. We reproduce its central claim as
our control, with seeds and error bars that the original comparison does not
report.
\item \textbf{LLM-Pruner} (Ma et al., 2023) establishes that depth-pruned
models require distillation to recover. We adopt that recipe, and we apply it
to \emph{both} of our operators so that neither is advantaged by being left
unrepaired.
\item \textbf{LayerDrop} (Fan et al., ICLR 2020) fine-tunes with structured
layer dropout. We run it as a full competing method rather than as a
baseline, and it turns out to be the strongest approach on one of our two
models.
\item \textbf{LLM in a 1.58 bits} (Ma et al., 2024) motivates the ternary
factor grid, though our negative result is about layer replacement rather than
weight quantization.
\item \textbf{Hinton et al. (2015)} for the distillation objective.
\end{itemize}
No closed-source large-LLM baselines were reproduced, per the instructions.

\subsection{Datasets, bias, and bottlenecks}
\paragraph{Corpus.} \texttt{Salesforce/wikitext}, configuration
\texttt{wikitext-2-raw-v1}, the standard layer-pruning benchmark. Its
non-raw and \texttt{wikitext-103} variants are available in the same
repository and were inspected as robustness checks.
\paragraph{Bias handling.} Preprocessing is identical across every operator:
non-overlapping fixed-length windows, the same evaluation token budget, and the
same calibration slice. The random-removal control is drawn from the
\emph{complement} of the BI target set, so a random configuration can never
accidentally receive the block we chose, making it a strictly fair null.
Target selection, calibration data, and evaluation data are disjoint in role
even though they come from the same corpus: $k$ is chosen on validation and
the test split is touched exactly once, afterwards.
\paragraph{Bottlenecks.} Datasets 5.x removed the script loaders and the
parquet mirrors for PTB and \texttt{tiny\_shakespeare} were not resolvable, so
we rotated the wikitext configurations instead. The principal compute
bottleneck was a single Apple MPS laptop, which is why four training-heavy
approaches initially ran at budgets too small to separate methods; those runs
were re-run at adequate budgets rather than reported.

\subsection{Experimental plan}
\paragraph{Models.} GPT-2 small (124,439,808 parameters, 12 blocks) and
SmolLM-135M (134,515,008 parameters, 30 blocks). They are architecturally
unlike each other: GPT-2 uses learned absolute position embeddings, GPT-2
attention, and \texttt{Conv1D} projections stored as $[\text{in},\text{out}]$,
whereas SmolLM uses RoPE, GQA and SwiGLU with projections stored as
$[\text{out},\text{in}]$. Getting that transposition wrong silently corrupts
GPT-2 position embeddings, so a thin \texttt{get\_layers}/\texttt{set\_layers}
helper normalises access.
\paragraph{How the approaches differ.} (i) \emph{target selection}: BI-ranked
versus random versus learned gates versus an EV-guided ranking; (ii) the
\emph{operator}: deletion, fitted ternary bridge, distillation repair,
LayerDrop, gate-masked deletion, or attention-head pruning; (iii)
\emph{scope}: $k$ from 1 to 3. Eight mechanism families are represented across
fifteen numbered approaches.
\paragraph{Hyperparameters and selection.} Bridge rank $r=32$, AbsMean ternary
grid (2 bits), ridge $10^{-4}$, Adam at $10^{-2}$ for 80 bridge-fitting steps.
Distillation: 400 steps, AdamW at $2\times10^{-5}$, temperature $T=2$,
$\alpha=0.5$ on the cross-entropy term, sequence length 256. LayerDrop:
500 steps, drop probability $0.15$. Learned gates: 300 steps, $\lambda=0.1$.
Layer selection is always the BI ranking unless the approach explicitly
varies it.
\paragraph{Metric.} Primary: validation perplexity
$\mathrm{PPL}=\exp(\frac{1}{N}\sum_i -\log p_\theta(x_i|x_{<i}))$ over
non-overlapping windows, identical for every operator. Secondary: parameter
count, fp16 byte count, and single-sequence latency. We additionally record
the BI value and the SVD explained-variance ceiling of every removed block.
\paragraph{System effort.} One MacBook with 16 GB RAM on the MPS backend for
the seeded studies, and a Colab T4 GPU for the final grid. Every configuration
is produced by \texttt{assignment2\_tpu.ipynb}, which is uploaded to Colab
and run end to end with a single \texttt{SMALL} flag selecting between the
verification pass and the paper grid.

\subsection{Project management}
\paragraph{Novelty proposal.} Test whether a fitted low-rank bridge can beat
plain deletion of the same block, and if it cannot, establish why with a
training-free diagnostic rather than by assertion.
\paragraph{Resources.} 1 laptop (16 GB, Apple MPS, no paid GPU) plus a Colab
T4 GPU runtime for the final grid.

\section{Experiment}

\subsection{Reproducibility, and a defect we had to fix first}
An earlier version of this study used a single global
\texttt{torch.manual\_seed} for the whole notebook. A seed audit demonstrated
that this made results depend on how many random numbers had been drawn
earlier in the run: the same configuration produced $47.67/27.04$ when
re-executed as its own cell and $47.95/27.67$ when reached by running the
notebook top to bottom. Those irreproducible figures appear in earlier drafts
of this report and have been removed everywhere.

The fix was to drive every stochastic fit from an explicit per-fit
\texttt{torch.Generator} rather than the global RNG. Healing additionally uses
a seed stream offset from the bridge-fitting stream so the two are not
correlated by accident. Section~\ref{sec:gate} reports the resulting
reproducibility check.

\paragraph{The bridge buys nothing.} The fitted bridge and plain removal
produce \emph{identical} parameter counts: $117{,}351{,}936$ for both at
GPT-2 $k{=}1$, against $124{,}439{,}808$ for the baseline. A rank-32 bridge is
$49{,}152$ weights standing in for a $7.1$M block, so it removes exactly the
same parameters as deletion and then adds a small perturbation. Latency is
likewise indistinguishable ($26.56$ ms for removal against $26.54$ ms for the
bridge on GPT-2). The bridge is therefore strictly dominated: it carries more
parameters-worth of risk and no benefit.

\subsection{Results: repair}
LLM-Pruner established that depth-pruned models need distillation, so
comparing two \emph{unrepaired} operators answers only ``which does less
damage''. We therefore ran the standard pruned-distillation objective
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \alpha\,\mathrm{CE}(y,\hat y_S) + (1-\alpha)\,T^2\,
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\mathrm{softmax}(\hat y_S/T)\,\middle\|\,
\mathrm{softmax}(\hat y_T/T)\right)
\end{equation}
with $S$ the pruned student and $T$ the frozen original. The $T^2$ factor is
not cosmetic: softening a 50k-way distribution by $T$ scales the gradient by
$1/T^2$, so omitting it silently downweights distillation until the run is
just fine-tuning. We assert that the KL term is non-trivial rather than
assuming it; the mean initial KL is $134.48$, falling to $34.81$.

On SmolLM-135M, LayerDrop at $k=1$ reaches $15.54 \pm 0.01$, which is
\emph{below} the $16.39$ unpruned baseline: deleting a block and fine-tuning
made the model better. This is the most surprising number in the study and we
report it rather than bury it, because it contradicts the obvious reading that
healing is the best available repair. It does not generalise: on GPT-2 the
same procedure reaches $51.74$, far worse than both plain deletion and
distillation. The honest conclusion is that \emph{the best method is
model-dependent}, and that a single recommended method would be an
overstatement of what this evidence supports.

\paragraph{Learned gates never beat BI.} Training a $L_0$ penalty on
per-block gates and then deleting the lowest-gate blocks reaches $44.25$ on
GPT-2 and $18.95$ on SmolLM, never better than the BI ranking that it is
supposed to improve on. The overlap between the learned gate ordering and the
BI ordering is exactly $0.00$ on GPT-2, i.e. the gate finds a completely
different set of blocks while arriving at a similar perplexity. Gates are a
more expensive way of rediscovering that most blocks are not equally
important.

\paragraph{Head pruning is a clean negative.} Pruning the least important
attention heads by magnitude is cheap at $12.5\%$ ($38.25\rightarrow42.85$ on
GPT-2) and catastrophic at $25\%$ ($2033.08$). On SmolLM even the $12.5\%$
setting is already severe ($16.36\rightarrow46.92$).

\subsection{Mechanism: why the bridge loses}
\paragraph{The diagnostic is training-free.} For each block we capture
calibration inputs $X$ and outputs $Y$, form the residual map
$\Delta = Y - X$, and compute its truncated-SVD explained variance. By
Eckart--Young this upper-bounds what \emph{any} rank-$r$ linear replacement can
achieve on that block, with no training at all.

\paragraph{Block influence and residual low-rankness are different
properties.} On GPT-2 the lowest-BI block is L4, whose rank-32 explained
variance is $0.392$, while L1 sits at $0.875$. On SmolLM the lowest-BI block
is L2 at $0.750$, against L27 at $0.445$. BI does not select the most linearly
compressible block, so an argument that conflates ``redundant'' with
``low-rank'' is simply wrong.

\paragraph{More rank makes perplexity worse, not better.} On GPT-2 the rank
sweep at $k=1$ gives $50.57$, $60.35$, $68.18$ and $341.10$ at ranks 4, 16,
64 and 128. The local fit improves monotonically while end-task perplexity
degrades monotonically. Layer-wise reconstruction error is therefore the wrong
optimisation target, which is the mechanistic explanation for the negative
result.

\paragraph{An honest weakness in this framing.} The bridge is rank-32
\emph{and} 2-bit, so ``low-rank replacement loses'' is entangled with
``ternary quantization hurts''. The bit ablation does not rescue the framing:
GPT-2 gives $52.35$ at 2 bits against $69.78$ at 4 and $69.72$ at 8, and
SmolLM gives $34.83$, $44.02$, $39.55$. More bits is monotonically \emph{worse},
which means quantization precision is not the bottleneck and we cannot
currently explain why 8 bits loses to 2. The defensible claim is therefore
narrower than the one we originally wrote: \emph{this particular
linear-plus-residual fitted form loses to deletion, and giving it more capacity
makes it worse.} Resolving this needs an unquantized float32 bridge at each
rank, which we have not run. These ablations are single-seed and should be
read as indicative rather than conclusive.

\subsection{Held-out test}
$k=1$ was selected on validation, so the test split is touched once, for that
configuration only. GPT-2: $53.89$ for BI removal against $67.97\pm10.65$ for
the bridge. SmolLM: $34.12$ against $50.33\pm1.24$. The ordering is preserved
from validation to test.

\subsection{Self-audit}\label{sec:gate}
The notebook ends with thirteen structural invariants and refuses to certify
its own numbers if any fails. They include bit-level reproducibility of the
fit, full seed coverage, monotone parameter counts, a random control provably
disjoint from the BI targets, finite perplexities, the SVD ceiling being a
genuine upper bound at matched rank, healing never increasing perplexity, the
healed model retaining the post-deletion parameter count, BI never losing to
random removal, and the held-out test perplexity tracking the unpruned model's
own validation-to-test gap rather than an arbitrary threshold. Three of these
checks initially failed and every failure was a defect in the check rather than
in the science: a seed-coverage expectation computed once for the whole table,
a rank ceiling read at the wrong rank, and an absolute validation-to-test
tolerance that would also have rejected a pristine model. We report this
because a gate that has never failed is not evidence of anything.

\subsection{Findings}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Best method is model-dependent.} BI removal plus distillation
wins on GPT-2 ($41.68$ against a $38.25$ baseline); LayerDrop wins on
SmolLM-135M and alone beats its own baseline ($15.54$ against $16.39$).
\item \textbf{A fitted low-rank bridge is dominated.} It loses to plain
deletion while achieving identical parameter counts and indistinguishable
latency.
\item \textbf{BI's advantage is reliability, not uniform magnitude.} BI
removal is never worse than random removal at any $(model,k)$, but at SmolLM
$k=1$ the two are within noise ($19.93$ against $20.16$). The advantage is
concentrated in the worst case: GPT-2 $k=1$ random removal reaches
$1453.93\pm2441.72$, with a standard deviation exceeding its own mean, which is
precisely the unreliability that makes a configuration undeployable.
\item \textbf{Block influence and residual low-rankness are different
properties}, and optimising the local fit actively harms perplexity.
\item \textbf{Learned gates and head pruning do not help.} Gates reach
similar perplexity by identifying a disjoint set of blocks; head pruning
fails at $25\%$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item Two models, both under 140M parameters. Nothing here is evidence about
7B-scale models, and depth-pruning behaviour is known to shift with scale.
\item One benchmark and one metric. \texttt{wikitext-2} perplexity only, with
no downstream task accuracy, so we cannot say whether these perplexity
differences would matter in use.
\item The bridge result is confounded with ternary quantization, as
Section~3.4 states plainly. The unquantized control is missing.
\item Ablations are single-seed.
\item Distillation is one untuned point: one learning rate, one temperature,
one step count, one $\alpha$. The recovery fractions in
Table~\ref{tab:heal} are lower bounds on what tuned distillation would give.
\item LayerDrop was run at one probability and one step count; given that it
produced the study's best result, a proper sweep is the highest-value
follow-up.
\item Latency is a single-sequence microbenchmark at one length, not a
serving benchmark.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
The defensible recommendation is to delete the blocks with the lowest Block
Influence and then repair with distillation, and to treat low-rank bridge
replacement as not worth the complexity at equal parameter count. The
stronger claim we set out to test, that a fitted bridge preserves perplexity
where deletion does not, is false, and we report it as false. The most
interesting result is not on our intended axis at all: LayerDrop fine-tuning
lands below the unpruned baseline on SmolLM-135M, which suggests that for
some architectures the right question is not how cheaply depth can be
removed but whether the surviving network can be improved once it is.
